The first 3D printed drug product approved by the FDA was Aprecia's Spritam (levetiracetam), announced in August 2015. Its ZipDose technology uses 3D printing to produce a porous tablet that disintegrates rapidly with a sip of liquid, and reviews from University College London (UCL) describe the process as a scaled-up form of binder jetting. Since then, **3D printed pharmaceutical pills personalized medicine** researchers have printed tablets with several drugs, tailored release profiles and patient-specific doses.

The technology has been demonstrated in the laboratory and in at least one small hospital study, and regulators in the UK and EU have started to address it directly.

---

## What Separates a Printed Pill from a Pressed Tablet

Most medicines are sold in a limited number of dose strengths and formulation types. A printed tablet is built layer by layer from a digital design, so its dose, shape and internal structure can be changed from one tablet to the next.

**Printlet** is a word coined by researchers at the UCL School of Pharmacy for a 3D printed solid oral dosage form.

The main technologies described in the UCL reviews:

| Technology | How it works | Reported use |
|---|---|---|
| Binder jetting | A liquid binder is selectively deposited over a powder bed | Highly porous, fast-dissolving tablets with high drug loading (the Spritam process) |
| Fused deposition modeling (FDM) | A thermal extrusion method that prints drug-loaded polymer filament | Tablets whose release rate depends on infill; sustained and immediate release |
| Semi-solid extrusion | A gel or paste is extruded from a syringe-like system in sequential layers, at lower temperatures than FDM | Chewable tablets and polypills with compartmentalised drugs |
| Stereolithography (SLA) | Light photopolymerises a liquid resin | Polypills containing up to six drugs |

## Custom-Release Formulations

### Sustained and Pulsatile Delivery

Printing lets the formulator change release behaviour through the design of the tablet. One review from the UCL group describes FDM tablets made with diltiazem as a model drug: by changing the infill percentage and the number of shells, both sustained and immediate-release tablets were obtained, and pulsatile and chrono controlled-release tablets were made by alternating placebo and drug-loaded filaments. The tablets were tested in rats, where release profiles were consistent with those obtained in vitro.

### Geometry as a Drug Delivery Tool

In an early UCL School of Pharmacy study, tablets were printed by FDM from polyvinyl alcohol filament loaded with fluorescein as a model drug, at infill settings from 0% to 100%. Lower infill gave faster release: tablets printed at 10% infill released their full load after 6 hours, while complete dissolution took 15 hours at 50% infill and 20 hours at 90% infill. Tablet weight also rose linearly with infill, which the authors noted could be used to control the dose.

## Multi-Drug Combination Pills: The Polypill Case

Polypharmacy can cause medication errors and non-adherence. A polypill combines several drugs in a single tablet, and 3D printing can place each drug in its own region of the tablet.

The best-known example comes from the University of Nottingham School of Pharmacy. In 2015 the group reported a five-in-one polypill made by 3D extrusion printing, with aspirin and hydrochlorothiazide in an immediate-release compartment and pravastatin, atenolol and ramipril in three sustained-release compartments.

UCL's group has printed a multi-layered polypill containing six drugs by stereolithography (2019). That study is also a caution: Raman microscopy showed that, although separate layers were printed, several of the drugs diffused across the layers.

A UCL review adds another caveat. Combining several medicines in one tablet can produce a large tablet, which is unfavourable for patients with dysphagia.

## Pediatric and Geriatric Dosage Personalization

### The Pediatric Dosing Problem

Children commonly need smaller, tailored doses based on body weight or body surface area. Because most medicines come in a few fixed strengths, patients and carers often crush or split tablets to reach the target dose, which carries a risk of dose variation.

The first clinical study to use 3D printing for personalised therapy in a hospital pharmacy was published in 2019. At the Hospital Clínico Universitario de Santiago de Compostela in Spain, four children with maple syrup urine disease received chewable isoleucine printlets made by semi-solid extrusion, in a single-centre, prospective, crossover study against conventional manually compounded capsules. The printed formulations kept isoleucine blood levels closer to the target value with less variability, and the different flavours and colours were generally well accepted.

### Geriatric Applications

Older patients may have swallowing impairments that affect which formulations they can take. Rapidly dispersing tablets, chewable formulations and orodispersible films can all be printed, and could ease administration for patients with dysphagia. According to its FDA label, Spritam disintegrates in the mouth in a mean time of 11 seconds (range 2 to 27 seconds) when taken with a sip of liquid.

## Drug Development and Clinical Trial Applications

The UCL reviews argue that printing could help early-phase development. On-demand printing could produce several product iterations for testing, and small batches of printlets with the desired dose could be made in a clinical setting immediately before administration, for example in first-in-human trials.

## Machine Learning for Formulation

Machine learning is being applied to the formulation step. In 2020 a team led from the UCL School of Pharmacy published M3DISEEN, a web-based tool for FDM printing built on 614 drug-loaded formulations made from 145 excipients. Its models predicted printability with 76% accuracy and filament characteristics with 67% accuracy, and predicted hot melt extrusion and FDM processing temperatures with mean absolute errors of 8.9 °C and 8.3 °C.

A 2021 follow-up mined 968 formulations from 114 published articles. Its best model, an artificial neural network, predicted drug release times with a mean error of about 24 minutes.

## Where the Technology Stands

In the UK, the Human Medicines (Amendment) (Modular Manufacture and Point of Care) Regulations 2025 came into force on 23 July 2025. The MHRA's overview lists 3D printed products that can only be printed at the point of care among its examples of point of care medicinal products.

In the EU, the European Medicines Agency's Quality Innovation Group held a focus group on personalised medicines in April 2025. Its meeting report records that 3D printing of medicines is already being used in pharmacies under compounding regulations, that certain envisioned routes for manufacture and supply do not fully fit the current EU regulatory framework, and that the group will continue working on guidance covering quality and manufacturing requirements.

Printing a tablet with a patient-specific dose and release profile has been demonstrated. Routine use depends on quality control and regulatory questions that are still being worked through.
